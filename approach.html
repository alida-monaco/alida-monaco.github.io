Our approach

A theory of change built on relationships.

Local issues are the wedge. Repeated, facilitated conversation is the method. Understanding is the goal.

Our posture

Understand, don’t convince.

We need to invest in conversations, not to convince, but to understand each other and to understand ourselves.

One organization cannot change the system. It can only invite the system to take responsibility for changing itself. This is not an effort to “convince” or “demonize” those who disagree about the climate crisis. It is an effort to engage, authentically, across belief systems to understand the shared cultural barriers to addressing it.

How cultural shifts happen

Four principles from adaptive leadership.

These ideas are rooted in Harvard Kennedy School Professor Ron Heifetz’s “Adaptive Leadership” framework, which describes how to invite a system to take responsibility for a problem it is avoiding, and how to handle the backlash that follows. Systems avoid collective problems for a reason, and difficult emotions often surface when they are asked to face them.

1. Invite, don’t impose

One organization cannot change the system. It can only invite the system to take responsibility for changing itself.

2. Build relational space

Our ability to honestly understand ourselves and each other depends on how well our relationships withstand uncomfortable emotions. We deliberately create environments where deep and unsettling differences can be held.

3. Repeat and be consistent

Value-system change happens by engaging in difficult conversations with the same people, repeatedly. Culture change takes time.

4. Encourage curiosity and self-reflection

We may not know why we hold certain beliefs, or how entwined they are with our identities. Understanding ourselves comes first.

Theory of change

Five steps from a local issue to lasting understanding.

  1. Identify the “wedge”

    Local, tangible environmental issues open a parting in the cloud of belief, a way into deep-rooted cultural identities. These become the anchors for conversation.

  2. Find the local “power wheel”

    Source the informal and formal leaders, stakeholders, and organizations that influence local customs and values.

  3. Build trust through local organizations

    Partner with local nonprofits, faith-based institutions, unions, companies, and other parties with a stake in the conversation.

  4. Create the container

    Invite power-wheel participants to meet for a facilitated conversation on the local issue, about two hours once a month for at least six months. The invitation is grounded in local stakes, framed as learning, and rooted in trust.

  5. Hold the container, repeatedly

    Expert facilitators moderate using adaptive leadership principles, again and again, with an impetus toward action in the community.

Our unique “wedge”: a local, tangible environmental issue opens a parting in the cloud of belief, a way in to the deep-rooted cultural identities at its core.

Three design requirements: informal and formal leaders are at the table; conversations center on local, tangible issues that serve as wedges to broader values; and facilitation is repeated over time with a bias toward action.

Precedent

How other institutions already do this.

Churches

The same congregation gathers repeatedly, over years, nurturing and reinforcing shared belief systems through sustained, deeply held engagement.

Colleges and universities

Students return to the same community for four years, long enough for the institution to shape identity and worldview through repeated contact.

What makes us different

Our unique value add.

Corporations that want to understand

We engage corporations seeking genuine understanding, not reputational cover.

A trusted, local-first brand

We work through local organizations to identify authentic, informal community leaders.

Facilitators who model the mindset

Our team is experienced in this exact work and brings the growth mindset the conversations require.

Working with others

Collaborators, not competitors.

Our goal is not to duplicate existing efforts, but to partner with local communities and existing efforts that share a common environmental goal, such as keeping a lake clean of trash and pollution. We can invite them to our conversations or integrate our programming through their local spaces.

  • Faith-based organizations encouraging environmental ethics conversations, such as Faith in Place, Interfaith Power and Light, the Evangelical Environmental Network, and Laudato Si’.
  • Academic forums connecting religion, ecology, and worldview, such as the Forum on Religion and Ecology at Yale.
  • Indigenous-led efforts focused on legal rights to, and interrelatedness with, ecosystems, such as White Earth, Ponca Nation, CELDF, and Movement Rights.
  • Traditional environmental nonprofits such as Sierra Club, The Nature Conservancy, the League of Conservation Voters, Environmental Defense Fund, and World Resources Institute work toward similar futures through legal, political, and thought-leadership levers. We don’t claim to wield a lever for change. We offer an invitation for people to wield their own.
  • Communication-focused groups like the Talking Climate Collaborative educate communicators to hold these conversations in their personal circles. We add the consistent, trust-building “container” for groups to work through them over time.
Integrity

Who can take part, and what we won’t do.

Nonprofits, NGOs, and companies who want to understand local sentiment in a real way may send a representative, in good faith, as a participant, and may pay a fee to do so.

Conversations for Change chooses the wedges it pursues, in line with its mission. Companies cannot pay us to set up a conversation as a way to persuade a community to accept a business venture. That would undermine the trustworthiness of the work and derail its authenticity.

What we’re still working through

Should we work with corporations? Being paid by corporations could affect our trustworthiness in local communities. How do we protect our authenticity and stay agenda-free?

How will we track impact? Cultural change is real, but notoriously hard to quantify. What metrics can responsibly capture progress?

We’d love your thinking. Tell us what you think.
